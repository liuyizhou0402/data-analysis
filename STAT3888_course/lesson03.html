Lecture 03

Unsupervised Learning: Introduction to Clustering

Finding groups in data when nobody tells you the labels.

STAT3888 · Statistical Machine Learning The University of Sydney · School of Mathematics & Statistics

Learning objectivesBy the end of this lecture you can…

  • Define the clustering problem and give nutrition examples.
  • Explain the role of a distance / dissimilarity measure.
  • Contrast within-cluster and between-cluster variation.
  • List the main clustering families and how their assumptions differ.

The problemWhat clustering does

From an unlabelled cloud (left) we propose a partition into groups (right). There is no ‘true’ answer to check against — clustering is exploratory.

Why cluster nutrition data?

Dietary patterns

Instead of studying 50 nutrients one at a time, group people into a few eating patterns (e.g. ‘prudent’ vs ‘Western’) and study those.

Metabolic subtypes

Cluster participants by metabolite profiles to reveal subgroups that respond differently — a hypothesis generator for the project.

Clustering is unsupervised: we use it to discover and describe structure, then validate biologically — not to predict a known label.

Everything hinges on distance

A clustering algorithm needs a way to say how far apart two observations are.

Euclidean:  d(x,y) = √ Σj (xj − yj)²

Common choices

Euclidean — straight-line, the default.

Manhattan — sum of absolute differences, robust.

Correlation — shape not magnitude (good for profiles).

Watch out

Distance is scale-sensitive — standardise first (Lecture 02!). The metric encodes what you mean by ‘similar’, so choose it deliberately.

The objectiveWhat makes a clustering ‘good’?

We want points close to their own centre (small within-cluster variation) and centres far apart (large between-cluster variation).

Total SS = Within + Between

The trade-off

Most algorithms minimise within-cluster variation. Adding clusters always reduces it — so we need principled ways to choose the number of clusters (Lecture 04).

In-class exerciseReason about distance

Two participants have daily intakes (energy kJ, vitamin C mg):

A = (8000, 60), B = (8000, 120), C = (16000, 60)

  1. Compute the Euclidean distance A–B and A–C on the raw scale.
  2. Which pair looks ‘closer’? Is that the answer you want?
  3. Recompute after standardising (assume SD = 4000 kJ and 30 mg). What changes, and why?
Hint. Raw distances are dominated by energy because its numbers are ~100× larger.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Clustering finds groups without labels — an exploratory, hypothesis-generating tool.
  • A distance measure defines ‘similar’; it is scale-sensitive, so standardise first.
  • Good clusterings have small within, large between variation.
  • Families differ by assumptions; we start with partitioning next.
