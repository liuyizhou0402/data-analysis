Lecture 04

K-means Clustering

The workhorse partitioning algorithm: how it works, how to tune it, and where it breaks.

STAT3888 · Statistical Machine Learning The University of Sydney · School of Mathematics & Statistics

Learning objectivesBy the end of this lecture you can…

  • State the k-means objective and describe Lloyd's algorithm.
  • Explain why k-means finds a local optimum and how random restarts help.
  • Choose the number of clusters with the elbow and silhouette methods.
  • Recognise the assumptions of k-means and when it fails.

The objective

K-means partitions n points into k clusters C1,…,Ck to minimise the total within-cluster sum of squares:

minC   Σk Σx∈Ck ‖ x − μk ‖²

In words

Each cluster is summarised by its centroid μk (the mean). We want every point close to its own centroid.

Why it's hard

Searching all partitions is NP-hard. Lloyd's algorithm gives a fast, greedy approximation that usually works well.

The algorithmLloyd's algorithm in action

Assign each point to the nearest centroid, then move each centroid to its points' mean; repeat until nothing changes. Here a poor start converged to a sub-optimal split — motivating random restarts.

The algorithm, step by step

Input: data X, number of clusters k.

  1. Initialise k centroids (randomly, or better with k-means++).
  2. Assign each point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.
  4. Repeat 2–3 until assignments stop changing.
Each step can only decrease the within-SS, so the algorithm always converges — but possibly to a local minimum that depends on the starting centroids.

Local optima &amp; how to beat them

The problem

Different random starts can give different clusterings. A bad start gets stuck (see the ‘Converged’ panel on the previous slide).

The fixes

Run k-means from many random starts and keep the best (lowest within-SS). Use k-means++ initialisation to spread starting centroids apart.

set.seed(1)
km <- kmeans(X, centers = 3, nstart = 25)  # 25 random restarts
km$tot.withinss   # objective value
km$cluster        # cluster labels

How many clusters?Choosing k: elbow &amp; silhouette

Left: within-SS always falls with k — look for the ‘elbow’ where the gain flattens. Right: the silhouette rewards well-separated clusters and often gives a clearer peak.

s(i) = ( bi − ai ) / max(ai, bi)

Reading silhouette

ai = mean distance to own cluster; bi = mean distance to nearest other cluster. Near +1 = well clustered, near 0 = on a boundary.

LimitationsKnow the assumptions — and the failure mode

K-means assumes roughly spherical, equal-size clusters and uses Euclidean distance. On these two interleaving ‘moons’ it carves a straight boundary and gets them wrong.

When k-means struggles

• Non-convex / elongated shapes

• Very different cluster sizes or densities

• Unscaled variables

→ Consider GMM (L05), hierarchical (L06) or density-based methods.

In-class exerciseRun and diagnose k-means

Using the standardised nutrition data:

  1. Fit k-means for k = 2,…,8 with nstart = 25 and record tot.withinss.
  2. Plot within-SS vs k. Where is the elbow?
  3. Compute the mean silhouette for each k (cluster::silhouette). Do the two methods agree?
  4. For your chosen k, profile the cluster means — can you give each cluster a nutrition name?
Hint. Naming clusters from their centroid profiles is exactly what you'll do in the project.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • K-means minimises within-cluster sum of squares via assign–update iterations.
  • It converges to a local optimum — always use many restarts / k-means++.
  • Choose k with the elbow and silhouette; let interpretability decide ties.
  • It assumes round, comparable clusters — standardise, and switch methods when that fails.
